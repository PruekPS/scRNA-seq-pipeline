QC Filtering — Method, Validation & Comparison to the Standard Threshold

A detailed look at scripts/01_qc_filter.py: what it computes, how the adaptive threshold works, and exactly how its output differs from the canonical fixed-threshold QC used in the standard PBMC3k tutorial — on the same data, numbers computed directly rather than estimated.

01 · Method

What the script actually computes

Three per-cell metrics, computed with scanpy's sc.pp.calculate_qc_metrics: total UMI counts, number of genes detected, and percent of counts from mitochondrial genes. Two hard floors are applied first — at least 200 genes per cell, at least 3 cells per gene — purely to drop the most obviously empty barcodes before any statistics are computed on the remaining population.

Adaptive (MAD) outlier detection

Rather than one fixed number applied to every dataset, each metric's acceptable range is derived from that dataset's own median and spread, following the approach formalized in OSCA3 and implemented in SCTK-QC1:

is_outlier(x) = x < median(x) − k·MAD(x) OR x > median(x) + k·MAD(x)
MAD(x) = median(|x − median(x)|)

Applied to log1p(total_counts), log1p(n_genes_by_counts), and pct_counts_mt, with k (the --mad flag) defaulting to 5 — inside OSCA's commonly-used 3–5 range. A hard mitochondrial ceiling (8% by default, --mt-ceiling) is applied in addition, as a backstop in case the adaptive bound lands somewhere unreasonably permissive on a given sample.

Doublet detection

Scrublet (via sc.pp.scrublet) flags predicted doublets independently of the MAD outlier logic — a cell can be a perfectly normal-looking single cell by counts/genes/mito and still get flagged here, or vice versa.

What gets removed

A cell is dropped from the output if it fails any of: counts outlier, genes outlier, mito outlier (adaptive or hard ceiling), or predicted doublet.

04 · Why they differ

Two concrete reasons, not a vague "more thorough"

  1. Classic has no lower bound. Past the initial 200-gene floor, the tutorial threshold never looks for unusually low-complexity cells — only unusually high genes or high mito. MAD is symmetric by construction, so it also catches the low tail (low counts, low genes) that the classic method structurally cannot see. That's the entire source of the 84 MAD-only cells above.
  2. Classic has no doublet detection. The Seurat PBMC3k tutorial predates widespread computational doublet calling. Scrublet adds 32 more flagged cells the classic method never looks for at all, by design rather than by omission.

One more detail worth flagging honestly: the MAD-derived gene ceiling (~1,820) came out stricter than the classic 2,500 on this dataset — the adaptive approach didn't just relax toward the old convention, it disagreed with it. That's expected: MAD reflects this dataset's actual spread, not a number chosen to work well across many datasets in general, which is precisely the tradeoff OSCA argues for3 and precisely why a fixed number picked for one dataset/tissue can be wrong for another.

05 · Caveats

What this comparison does not establish

Not yet validated on real project data

PBMC3k is droplet-based (10x) data, used here purely as a public smoke test because it's the only thing available so far — see pipeline_overview.html section 05 for why split-pool (Parse) doublet and ambient-RNA mechanisms differ from droplet platforms. The MAD thresholds and the classic-vs-adaptive gap shown here are specific to this dataset's own distribution; they will look different on real Evercode data, and should be re-run and re-reported once that data exists rather than assumed to transfer.

"Standard" here means one specific convention

The 200–2500 genes / 5% mito rule is the most widely cited convention for exactly this dataset, not a universal standard — other published PBMC3k analyses use other cutoffs. The comparison above is precise about which standard it's checked against, not a claim of universal agreement.
